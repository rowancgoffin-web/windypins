WindyPins, Manual

This manual describes the data sources, formulas, and approximations used to calculate annual energy production (AEP), section by section.

1. Scope

A screening-level energy yield estimator: turbines are placed on a map and an indicative annual energy production is calculated. It is not a bankable assessment. Terrain is handled by a linear flow model rather than CFD, no measured wind data is used, and uncertainty is a simplified build-up rather than the full statistical analysis of an Energy Yield Assessment (EYA). Outputs indicate whether a site merits further investigation, not whether it would be financeable.

2. Wind data sources

This tool uses two wind data sources:

  • Global Wind Atlas (GWA) generalized wind climate (GWC) files (the normal resource path): sector-wise frequency and Weibull A/k parameters at the site centre (§2b), retrieved via the wind-stats Python library.
  • NASA POWER (fallback, used only when the GWA lookup fails): a MERRA-2 reanalysis-derived climatology, accessed via a REST API with no registration required.

Both are gridded products. MERRA-2 is a reanalysis: observations (satellites, radiosondes, surface stations, ships and buoys) are assimilated into a global atmospheric model to produce wind fields on a regular grid. GWA further downscales reanalysis-scale wind statistics using terrain and roughness models to a finer effective resolution, though the GWC files served here represent the generalized (not site-specific) regional climate (see §5).

Extracting a value at a specific site from NASA POWER involves:

  1. Locating the nearest grid cell (~50 km resolution, 0.5°×0.625°). Nearest-neighbour selection is used. Bilinear interpolation across the four surrounding grid points would remove the artificial step discontinuities between cells.
  2. Selecting the vertical level closest to hub height: NASA POWER publishes wind at 2m, 10m, and 50m, not at hub height directly, so an extrapolation is needed (§3).
  3. Aggregating to an annual climatological mean from the twelve monthly values NASA POWER's climatology endpoint returns.

NASA POWER is used specifically because it requires no API key or registration and returns a REST response in a single call.

2a. ERA5 hourly statistics (v12)

A third source, ERA5 (ECMWF reanalysis, ~31 km, hourly, accessed via the Open-Meteo archive API without registration), supplies temperature and long-term temporal statistics at the site centre. It is not used as the wind resource baseline. This was tested directly: fitting Weibull parameters to 20 years of hourly ERA5 100 m wind and running them through the yield pipeline reproduced London Array's implied wind speed to within roughly +9%, but came out ~16% low at Braes of Doune, a 400 m upland site whose ridge the ~31 km cell averages together with the surrounding valleys. Terrain smoothing of that magnitude, cubed through a power curve, is disqualifying for onshore resource estimation, so GWA remains the resource path (§5).

ERA5 is instead used where synoptic-scale data is appropriate or where hourly chronology is essential:

  • Mean temperature for air density (§3b): the mean 2 m temperature over the record, which Open-Meteo has already adjusted to ground level at the site centre.
  • Interannual variability (§10), per-year annual means from the hourly record. IAV is a regional quantity, so grid resolution is not a limitation here.
  • Seasonal energy profile: monthly mean 100 m wind aggregated over the record, displayed as each month's share of annual energy. Only the seasonal shape is taken from ERA5, and the annual level remains GWA-based, consistent with the resource-role division above.
  • Icing screening (§6). The fraction of hours with hub-height temperature below 0°C coincident with relative humidity above 96%, a criterion that monthly climatology cannot resolve.
  • High-wind hysteresis (§6), counting post-cut-out deadband hours requires the chronological sequence of the wind record, not its distribution, so a Weibull fit cannot produce it.

Weibull parameters reported by the ERA5 endpoint are fitted by maximum likelihood to the hourly sample, excluding calms below 0.5 m/s (the log-likelihood is undefined at zero, and such hours are below every cut-in speed in the library). The exclusion biases the fit slightly high: ~+0.5% on A and ~+1.5% on k at k = 2, growing at lower k (~+2%/+4% at k = 1.7). These fields are retained for diagnostics and comparison against GWA, not used in the production pipeline.

Hub-height temperature for the icing criterion is estimated from ERA5's 2 m temperature with a standard 6.5 K/km environmental lapse rate. Relative humidity is taken at 2 m without correction, which under-counts icing hours slightly (RH generally increases with height in the boundary layer), making the criterion conservative.

2b. Site centre

Every calculation is anchored to one site centre, which fixes the GWA cell used for the wind rose and the ERA5 point used for temperature and the long-term statistics. It follows the layout automatically: the centroid of the sited turbines, or the centroid of a drawn site boundary when there are no turbines yet. With neither, there is no centre.

Placing the centre and fetching from it are separate. The marker moves with the layout at no cost, while the GWA and ERA5 lookups commit at three points only: the first turbine, drawing a boundary, and leaving the Siting tab with the layout settled. A commit is skipped if the centre has moved less than 100 m since the last one, which is below the GWA grid spacing and so cannot change the resource cell. Loading a site file commits once, after the load.

Clicking "Click to set site centre" and then the map pins the centre where it was put, whatever later happens to the layout. "Use turbine centroid" returns it to automatic. A pinned centre survives the removal of every turbine, while an automatic one goes with the last turbine or boundary so the next site starts clean. Site files record which mode was in use, and files saved before v15 load as pinned so their results reproduce exactly.

If the centre sits more than 20 km from the turbine centroid or the boundary, the tool warns that the wind rose may not represent that location rather than blocking the run.

3. Wind shear

Wind speed increases with height. Two standard extrapolation methods are available (Shearing tab), both applying to the NASA POWER fallback path only. On the normal GWA path the wind rose is already height-interpolated server-side and neither method is applied on top of it.

Power law (default):

V(h) = V(h_ref) × (h / h_ref)^α

α cannot be derived for a specific site without multi-height wind measurements, so a fixed, user-adjustable value is used. Typical values: 0.10–0.12 offshore or flat terrain, 0.14 open onshore, 0.20–0.30+ forested or urban.

Log law (alternative, selectable in the Shearing tab):

V(h) = V(h_ref) × ln(h / z₀) / ln(h_ref / z₀)

z₀ is the surface roughness length, set in the Shearing tab and shared with the GWA sector interpolation (§5). The log law is the standard alternative to the power law and is generally considered more physically grounded near the surface, within the atmospheric boundary layer's log-linear region. The power law is a simpler empirical fit that is easier to parameterise without a measured roughness length. The two methods agree reasonably well over the height ranges typical of onshore wind turbines and diverge more at greater heights or over rougher terrain. z₀ = 0 ("Water") is clamped to 0.0002 m for this calculation, since the log law is undefined at z₀ = 0.

Both methods extrapolate from the same NASA POWER 50m climatological mean. Neither derives a location-specific exponent or roughness length from the reanalysis data itself, and neither captures diurnal or atmospheric-stability effects, which multi-level in-situ measurement does. Deriving α from two reanalysis heights at the same grid cell (ERA5's 10m and 100m levels, say) would give a location-varying estimate instead of one number applied everywhere.

Measured wind speeds at height (advanced, Shearing tab). Where measured speeds at two or more heights are available (met mast, LiDAR), the shear exponent can be fitted from them instead of assumed: α is the least-squares slope of ln(v) against ln(h). The fitted value is applied to the power law and shown with the number of points used. This feeds the same fallback-only path as the rest of the tab. Fits outside the physical range (α ≤ 0 or > 1) are rejected rather than applied.

The roughness field (z₀) does four separate jobs depending on path, worth stating plainly in one place:

  1. Log-law shear (this section), NASA POWER fallback path only.
  2. GWA sector interpolation (§5): the roughness class the GWC data is re-specialised to. GWA path only, and independent of the shear-law setting above, since the two paths never run together.
  3. Wake turbulence intensity (§4), which sets the Gaussian wake's growth rate on the GWA path (TI ≈ 1/ln(h/z₀)). This is a different physical quantity from (2) that happens to depend on the same field.
  4. Terrain model (§11), where it sets the depth of the inner layer and so how strongly the flow near the ground responds to hills. Both paths.

One field, four uses, two or three active depending on which path is running. Set it to the roughness that actually matches the site regardless of which path you expect to be active, since it silently matters to whichever one is.

3a. Weibull k estimation (NASA POWER fallback path)

On the NASA POWER fallback path, the Weibull shape parameter k is estimated per location via the Justus & Mikhail (1976) relation:

k ≈ (σ / mean)^-1.086

applied to the coefficient of variation of the twelve monthly climatological means (NASA POWER's climatology endpoint does not provide sub-monthly data). This relation is normally applied to the standard deviation of the actual (hourly or sub-daily) wind speed distribution. Using monthly-mean variability as a proxy captures the seasonal cycle only, not the larger synoptic and diurnal variability that dominates the true value of k, and tends to bias k high (distribution narrower than reality). The GWA path, which is the normal case, provides real per-sector k values from GWC data and does not carry this limitation.

3b. Air density correction

Power curves are defined at standard sea-level density (1.225 kg/m³). Each turbine's density is worked out at its hub, from the ground elevation in the terrain grid (§11) plus the hub height:

p = 101325 × (1 − 2.25577×10⁻⁵ × z)^5.25588
ρ = p / (287.05 × T)

where z is the hub's altitude and T is the mean temperature there. T comes from the site's ERA5 record (§2a): the mean 2 m temperature, which Open-Meteo has already adjusted to ground level at the site centre, lapsed up to each hub at 6.5 K/km. If ERA5 is unavailable the standard atmosphere is used instead (15 °C at sea level), which runs about 5 °C warm for UK sites and puts density around 2% low.

Density acts inside the power curve (§13a), on the cube term only. Output at rated power is unaffected, which is how pitch-regulated machines behave, so a high site loses less than its density ratio suggests. Before v15 the whole AEP was scaled by the density ratio, and that ratio used the pressure exponent rather than the density one. At a 400 m site the two together understated energy by about 3.5%.

Humidity is not modelled, and neither is the tendency for strong winds to come with colder, denser air. Both effects are small.

4. Wake model

The wake model is the Gaussian velocity-deficit model of Bastankhah & Porté-Agel (2014), the profile modern industry tools default to, with turbulence-derived wake growth and root-sum-square multi-wake combination.

Velocity deficit

A turbine of rotor diameter D₀ generates a wake whose fractional velocity deficit at downwind distance x, crosswind offset y, and hub-height offset Δz is:

δ(x, y, Δz) = C(x) · exp(−(y² + Δz²) / 2σ(x)²)

σ(x)/D₀ = k*·x/D₀ + 0.2·√β,    β = ½ (1 + √(1−Ct)) / √(1−Ct)
C(x)    = 1 − √(1 − Ct / (8(σ/D₀)²))

σ is the Gaussian wake width, growing linearly with distance at rate k*, and C is the centreline deficit. Because the profile is Gaussian in crosswind distance, partial waking falls out of the formula directly. A turbine offset from the wake centreline receives a proportionally reduced deficit, with no separate overlap-area geometry needed. The Δz term handles fleets with mixed hub heights. The far-wake formula over-predicts sharply near its validity boundary (x ≲ 1.9D₀ at onshore turbulence), so C is capped at the actuator-disc initial deficit 1 − √(1−Ct), the physically attainable maximum.

Wake growth from turbulence

Wake recovery is driven by atmospheric turbulence, which in the neutral surface layer follows from the surface roughness length: TI ≈ 1/ln(h/z₀), with h the upstream turbine's hub height. The growth rate then follows the Niayifar & Porté-Agel (2016) empirical relation:

k* = 0.003678 + 0.3837 · TI

Representative values: onshore (z₀ = 0.03 m, h = 80 m) TI ≈ 12.7%, k* ≈ 0.052. Open water (z₀ = 0.0002 m, h = 90 m): TI ≈ 7.7%, k* ≈ 0.033. Lower offshore turbulence means wakes recover more slowly and persist further downwind. This ties wake behaviour to the roughness the user already sets (§5) rather than a separate hardcoded constant. The "Offshore site" checkbox (Siting tab) forces the open-water roughness in this calculation regardless of the roughness selection. The exported report states the k* and TI used.

Direction handling

Where a wind rose is available, the calculation runs per sector, and within each sector at five sub-bearings spanning the sector's width. For each sub-bearing, every neighbour's position is decomposed relative to the wind axis into a downwind distance x (neighbours downwind of the subject turbine cannot wake it) and crosswind offset y, and the Gaussian deficit evaluated. Averaging across sub-bearings represents partial-time waking: a wake narrower than a full 30° sector only counts for the fraction of that sector's wind directions it actually covers. Geometry is continuous in both position and bearing, small turbine moves produce small, smooth changes in wake loss.

Where no rose is available, because the GWA lookup failed, a deliberately conservative omnidirectional worst case is used: every neighbour is treated as directly upwind on the wake centreline simultaneously.

Multi-wake combination

Overlapping deficits from multiple upstream turbines combine as the root-sum-square of the individual deficits (Katić et al. 1986), capped at 0.99:

δ_total = √(Σᵢ δᵢ²)

Interior turbines in a large array therefore feel every upstream row, not only the strongest single wake.

Fixed parameters and residual simplifications

  • Thrust coefficient varies with wind speed. Ct is ~0.8 below rated output (typical for pitch-regulated machines) and rolls off above it as blade pitch sheds load: Ct(v) = 0.8·(v_knee/v)² above the knee (the speed where the power curve reaches ~95% of rated), floored at 0.05, giving ~0.18 at 25 m/s for a 12 m/s knee, consistent with published curves ending around 0.1–0.15. Rather than integrating the deficit per wind-speed bin, each sector uses an effective Ct: the Weibull-weighted mean of Ct(v) over that sector's own wind distribution, computed once per sector per upstream turbine class. Sectors with more high-wind hours therefore produce weaker wakes, correcting the previous constant-Ct overstatement. The no-rose fallback path retains the flat 0.8, consistent with its worst-case philosophy.
  • No wake-added turbulence: deep-array effects, where upstream wakes raise TI and accelerate the recovery of downstream wakes, are not modelled.
  • Blockage: an editable allowance in loss 1c, default 2%, with a vortex-cylinder rotor-induction estimate shown beside it (see Blockage effect in §6).

4a. External wake, neighbouring farms (loss 1b)

From v13, wakes from turbines outside the user's layout can be computed rather than manually entered. Neighbouring turbines are retrieved from OpenStreetMap (via the Overpass API, backend-cached) within a user-set radius, defaulting to 30 km onshore and 50 km offshore, reflecting the greater persistence of offshore cluster wakes. Retrieval is always explicit: a fetch button in the Siting tab, never a background action, and the result line states every decision taken: turbines found, turbines excluded as the user's own, turbines used, and how many carry assumed geometry.

Self-filtering. Querying from inside or near one's own (real, mapped) site returns the user's own turbines as neighbours, and counting them would double internal wake as external. Any OSM turbine within max(200 m, 1.5× rotor diameter) of a placed turbine is treated as the same machine and excluded. Deliberately placing a subset of a real farm therefore leaves the remainder correctly counted as external.

Assumed geometry. OSM frequently omits rotor diameter and almost always omits hub height. Missing values default to a 110 m rotor and 90 m hub (100 m when the offshore toggle is set), representative of the fleet built in the last ~15 years. Affected turbines are flagged in the result line and their map tooltips. Wake interaction is dominated by rotor diameter and thrust, with hub-height error entering only as a second-order vertical offset, so the assumption is deliberately coarse.

Model integration. External turbines enter the identical Bastankhah–Porté-Agel mathematics as fleet turbines, with the same growth rate and the same sector-effective thrust treatment (a generic modern spec stands in for their Ct knee), but produce no energy. Pairs beyond 25 km are skipped as negligible. The 1a/1b split is kept honest by a two-pass computation: internal-only wake gives 1a, internal-plus-external gives the total, and 1b is the difference expressed against post-1a energy, composing correctly in the multiplicative sequence. While neighbours are included, the manual 1b entry is disabled and shows the calculated value, and clearing or excluding them restores it.

Empty results and radius. A search that returns nothing is a result, not a failure: the tool reports that no turbines were found and sets loss 1b to zero, rather than leaving a previously typed value in place. Radius is selectable from 5 to 50 km in 5 km steps, but the physics does not reward the upper end: an individual Gaussian wake has decayed to nothing well before 20 km (pairs beyond 25 km are skipped outright), so wider searches mainly add map context for verifying OpenStreetMap coverage more than it changes the calculated loss.

Known limitations. Individual Gaussian wakes decay to nothing over 10–15 km, so distant neighbouring farms (e.g. ~20 km away) correctly compute a 1b of ~0% under this model, yet measured offshore cluster wakes can persist at such ranges as a mesoscale phenomenon this pairwise treatment cannot represent. A distant-cluster 1b of zero is the model working as designed, not evidence of no effect, so judge such cases manually. Separately, OSM completeness varies by region: an unmapped neighbour contributes nothing and produces no warning, an invisible underestimate. The mitigation is visibility not trust: fetched neighbours render on the map in a distinct hollow style for verification against local knowledge, and individual turbines can be removed (mapping errors, decommissioned machines) but not edited, since they are external data rather than fleet.

5. Wind rose and roughness

A wind rose requires a joint distribution of speed and direction, typically 12 or 16 sectors, each with its own frequency and Weibull A/k. NASA POWER's climatology endpoint returns only a single annual mean and cannot produce one. The rose comes from Global Wind Atlas GWC files, which contain sector-wise frequency and Weibull A/k directly, retrievable via the wind-stats Python library. If the backend cannot be reached, the rose shown is a deterministic synthetic function of coordinates, labelled "SYNTHETIC" in the widget. Binning a reanalysis time series by direction would be the alternative rose source, at the cost of substantially more data per point.

GWC data does not describe the wind at the exact query point as-is. It describes the generalized regional wind climate: the wind statistics the region would have if its surface were uniformly one of several standard roughness classes, tabulated at several standard heights. This is the standard input format for microscale modelling (WAsP-style), and the downstream tool re-specialises the generalized climate to local conditions by interpolating to the actual hub height and the roughness class matching the site's real surroundings. That interpolation is what the roughness setting (Shearing tab) controls on the GWA path, and it has a first-order effect on yield. Physically, for the same regional (geostrophic) driving wind, a rougher surface extracts more momentum from the flow, so hub-height wind is lower over rough terrain than smooth: moving the class from 0.03 m (open farmland) to 0.1 m (scattered obstacles) at a ~70 m hub typically lowers hub-height wind by several percent, and since energy responds roughly cubically in the operating range, gross yield can move 15–20%. Vertical shear is applied exactly once in this path, inside the GWC height/roughness interpolation, and the Shearing tab's shear-law setting is not applied on top of it. Pick the roughness class that matches the site's actual surface, and treat the choice with the same seriousness as hub height. The effect is minimal offshore (water is a single class, 0.0 m), and onshore it is one of the largest levers in the tool. Hills and valleys are added back per turbine by the flow model of §11, which is why the generalized climate, not GWA's own terrain-adjusted map, is the right input.

Note that the GWC lookup is a point query at the site-centre coordinates: it returns the generalized climate at that location, not an area summary. It therefore does not inherit the selection bias of area statistics such as "value for the windiest fraction of the area" reported by some atlas interfaces. The generalized climate varies smoothly, on the scale of the mesoscale model behind GWA (a few kilometres), so exactly where the centre sits within a site matters little. Local terrain enters per turbine through §11.

6. Loss assumptions

As of v12, loss categories follow the IEC 61400-15-2 loss framework: six top-level categories (wake effect, availability, electrical, turbine performance, environmental, curtailment) plus an "other" line. The 1a/1b/… numbering used in the interface is this tool's presentation of that taxonomy, not an official IEC clause numbering. Sub-items a screening user rarely adjusts (parasitic consumption, site-specific power-curve adjustment, long-term degradation, operational-strategy curtailment) are collapsed behind an Advanced disclosure per category. A collapsed field's value is always applied, and the disclosure opens automatically whenever its value differs from the default, so nothing is hidden while active.

Losses are applied multiplicatively, in sequence, to whatever energy remains after prior stages, standard EYA practice, avoiding double-counting. The Results tab and Per-turbine results tab both show this as an explicit waterfall (loss %, MWh impact, remaining yield per stage).

Defaults are DNV-typical onshore values. They are generic, not project-specific: production numbers should come from O&M contracts (availability), grid connection agreements (curtailment), and environmental surveys (icing, degradation) for a given project. The default set retains the pre-v12 multiplicative total to within ~0.3 percentage points. The difference is the addition of parasitic consumption (3c, 0.1%) and blade soiling / leading-edge erosion (5b, 0.2%) as explicit small defaults.

Save-file compatibility. Site files written before v12 carry only the old category keys. On load, sub-items introduced in v12 are zeroed before the saved values are applied, so a pre-v12 file reproduces its original waterfall total exactly rather than silently gaining the new defaults on top. Files saved from v12 onward carry a schema marker (lossSchema: 2) and restore all fields directly.

Computed decision-support values (icing, hysteresis)

Two environmental/performance entries are supported by values computed from the ERA5 hourly record (§2a). In both cases the computed figure informs the user-entered loss and never overwrites it.

Icing (5a/5c). The interface reports the fraction of the 20-year record with estimated hub-height temperature below 0°C coincident with RH > 96%, against the 1%-of-hours screening threshold used in the cold-climate literature (after IEA Wind Task 19 practice) as a trigger for icing uncertainty quantification. The tool deliberately does not convert this to an ice class or a loss percentage: published validation work cautions against mechanical use of the class-to-loss translation, and the mapping from icing hours to AEP loss depends on rotor technology, blade heating, and shutdown strategy. The screen answers "does icing need attention at this site". The loss values remain the user's judgement. As a live cross-check, the criterion returns 0.002% of hours at London Array (Thames Estuary) and 1.69%, above threshold, at Braes of Doune (upland Stirlingshire), matching physical expectation.

High-wind hysteresis (4b). After a cut-out trip, control systems hold the turbine offline until wind falls to a restart threshold typically 3–5 m/s below cut-out. Hours spent inside that deadband following a trip are in-limits but non-producing. This is computed by walking the chronological ERA5 hourly series with a two-state machine (producing / tripped), counting in-limits hours lost to the deadband. Deadband wind speeds are above rated for every turbine in the library, so lost energy is rated power × lost hours, expressed against AEP at the fleet's current net capacity factor. Validity is offshore/flat terrain only: ERA5's ~31 km cell smooths the terrain-accelerated gusts that trip cut-out in complex terrain, at Braes of Doune the record contains zero hours above 25 m/s, which is a resolution artefact, not a site property. For windy onshore sites the interface directs the user to the industry-typical 0.1–0.5% band instead. At London Array the computed value is ~0.07% of AEP (50 deadband hours in 20 years), slightly below that band, consistent with ERA5 under-resolving the extreme tail even offshore.

Blockage effect

Wake models, including the Bastankhah–Porté-Agel Gaussian model used here (§4), and the Jensen model before it, historically did not account for turbines slowing the wind upstream of themselves and the array as a whole. DNV GL's 2018 analysis (Bleeg et al., Energies 11, 1609) identified this as a source of systematic AEP overestimation across the industry, and DNV's figures put the previously unaccounted blockage loss at 0–4% of AEP depending on layout and turbine characteristics.

Calculated estimate (v13). A physically modelled figure now accompanies the band. The closed-form vortex-cylinder solution (after Branlard & Meyer Forsting) gives the centreline velocity deficit at upstream distance x of a rotor of radius R as a·(1 − x/√(x²+R²)), with induction a = (1−√(1−Ct))/2 at Ct = 0.8. Lateral decay is approximated as Gaussian with σ = R. Contributions superpose linearly across the fleet (exact for ideal vortex cylinders), sectors are frequency-weighted from the wind rose, and the fleet-mean velocity deficit converts to energy via the cubic linearisation ΔE/E ≈ 3·Δu/u, valid at the sub-2% deficits involved and far cheaper than a further full integration pass. This captures rotor-induced blockage only: measured blockage includes an atmospheric/global component the formulation cannot represent, so the calculated figure is a documented lower bound. It is shown beside loss 1c for reference. In v13 and v14 it was applied automatically, but at normal turbine spacings it comes out close to zero (0.02% at London Array), which took blockage out of every result. Loss 1c is again an editable allowance, defaulting to 2%, with the DNV 0–4% band as the guide.

Uncertainty and exceedance (P50/P75/P90/P99)

Net AEP is treated as the P50, and P75, P90 and P99 follow from an overall uncertainty under a normal distribution. The overall figure is suggested from a component build-up (resource, wake, power curve and interannual variability) and can be edited. §10 has the details. It is a simplified treatment, not a substitute for a project-specific uncertainty budget.

7. Offshore applicability

The underlying data pipeline covers open water: NASA POWER includes ocean grid points, and reanalysis wind quality over water benefits from assimilated satellite scatterometer and altimeter observations.

  • Wake growth: the offshore toggle forces open-water roughness in the turbulence-based wake growth (§4), so offshore wakes recover more slowly.
  • Terrain: not modelled offshore, since open water is flat. With the toggle off, coastal turbines still get the terrain model, with the sea treated as flat ground.
  • Turbine library: includes eight offshore classes spanning 3.6 MW to 20 MW (up to a 260 m rotor, §13).
  • Shear: offshore shear is typically lower and flatter (α ≈ 0.10–0.12) than onshore. It is adjustable in the Shearing tab but not defaulted differently by mode.
  • Blockage: DNV's research found blockage affects both onshore and offshore projects, with magnitude driven by turbine/layout characteristics rather than the onshore/offshore distinction, and the same blockage allowance (§6) applies to both.
  • Not modelled: array losses specific to 50–100+ turbine offshore farms, foundation/marine O&M cost structures, and seabed constraints. Water depth is available as an EMODnet/GEBCO map layer for visual screening, but does not enter any calculation.
  • Map tiles: all available basemaps render open water without issue.

8. Known limitations

  • The terrain model (§11) is linear. It overstates speed-ups on steep ground and cannot represent flow separation in the lee of hills, where measured speeds fall well below its prediction. RIX (§12) flags where this matters.
  • Outside a few countries the terrain data is a surface model at about 30 m, so plantation forestry reads as terrain and narrow summits are rounded off.
  • Roughness is a single value for the whole site and every direction. There is no forest canopy or displacement height modelling, and no atmospheric stability dependence.
  • The wind rose is real (GWA) when the backend is reachable and a labelled synthetic placeholder otherwise.
  • The wake model's Ct(v) roll-off is a generic analytic shape, not a manufacturer thrust curve, and wake-added turbulence (deep-array recovery effects) is not modelled.
  • Blockage is a single user-set allowance (§6). The calculated figure shown beside it covers rotor induction only.
  • Weibull k on the NASA POWER fallback path is a heuristic from seasonal variability (§3a).
  • Air density uses the site's mean temperature, without humidity or any link between temperature and wind speed (§3b).
  • Uncertainty and exceedance use a single-number, normal-distribution treatment, not a full component-by-component budget.
  • Power curves are a generic shape fitted to manufacturer curves (§13a), not the curve of a specific machine.
  • Grid fill (§13b) places turbines by geometry alone, with no wake feedback, exclusion zones, setbacks or slope limits. Boundaries crossing the antimeridian are not supported.

9. Energy integration mathematics

Annual energy per turbine is the expectation of the power curve over the wind speed distribution:

AEP (MWh/yr) = 8760/1000 × ∫₀^∞ f(v) · P(v) dv

where P(v) is the power curve (§13a) and f(v) is the Weibull probability density:

f(v) = (k/A) · (v/A)^(k−1) · exp(−(v/A)^k)

A (scale, m/s) sets the distribution's magnitude and k (shape, dimensionless, typically 1.5–2.5 onshore) sets its width, with lower k meaning more spread. The distribution's mean relates to A through the gamma function:

mean = A · Γ(1 + 1/k)

so when only a mean wind speed is known (the NASA POWER path), A is recovered as A = mean / Γ(1 + 1/k) using a Lanczos-series gamma implementation (accurate to ~1e−13 over the range used). The integral is evaluated numerically with a fixed step of dv = 0.25 m/s from 0 to max(30, cut-out + 1) m/s, introducing well under 1% quadrature error against the smooth power curve.

On the GWA path, this integration runs per sector: each of the 12 sectors contributes freqᵢ × E[P | Aᵢ, kᵢ], with the sector's A first scaled by that sector's terrain speed-up (§11) and the power curve evaluated at the turbine's hub height air density (§3b). Wake is applied per sector by scaling that sector's A by (1 − deficit) before the integration, shifting the whole distribution down, consistent with a wake being a wind-speed (not power) deficit.

The reported per-turbine "mean hub-height wind speed" is the frequency-weighted mean of the sector Weibull means, Σᵢ freqᵢ · Aᵢ · Γ(1 + 1/kᵢ), after terrain speed-up and before wake. A familiar benchmark figure, though note that two sites with the same mean can differ meaningfully in energy if their k differs.

10. Uncertainty and exceedance mathematics

Net AEP is treated as the P50 (median) of a normal distribution whose standard deviation is the overall uncertainty σ (%). Exceedance values follow directly from normal quantiles:

P_x = P50 × (1 − z_x · σ)      z₇₅ = 0.6745, z₉₀ = 1.2816, z₉₉ = 2.3263

The displayed P90/P50 ratio is a restatement of the uncertainty value: with σ = 12%, P90/P50 = 1 − 1.2816 × 0.12 ≈ 0.85, always. It is shown because it is the figure lenders and analysts habitually quote, but it contains no information the uncertainty value didn't already.

Component build-up

The overall σ is suggested (and auto-filled until manually edited) as the root-sum-square of four components:

Component Basis
Wind resource 9% on the GWA direction-weighted path and 13% on the NASA POWER fallback, which has a coarser ~50 km grid, no rose and a heuristic Weibull k (§3a). 3% more is combined in (RSS) if any sited turbine's RIX exceeds 5% (§12), where the terrain model is least reliable.
Wake model 3% floor + 15% of the calculated wake loss. The standard EYA convention that wake uncertainty scales with wake magnitude, so a lightly-waked site carries little wake uncertainty and a heavily-waked one carries more.
Power curve 3% flat. The fitted generic curve (§13a) matches manufacturer curves to about 1.3% RMS, but one Cp_max for every machine carries more uncertainty than that.
Interannual variability Computed per site where possible (below), with a literature fallback otherwise. User-overridable.

Both the IAV and the overall value are editable, and a manual edit stops the auto-fill for that field. The component line is displayed alongside so the number is traceable rather than asserted.

Interannual variability (IAV)

The site's IAV is computed rather than assumed, from the same 20-year ERA5 record as §2a (~31 km grid). If ERA5 is unavailable, NASA POWER's monthly record (MERRA-2 derived, ~50 km grid, from 1984) is used instead.

  1. Year-by-year annual mean wind speeds are assembled from the record, at 100 m for ERA5 and 50 m for NASA POWER. Incomplete years are skipped.
  2. The series is detrended (a linear fit is removed, residuals kept around the series mean) so documented long-term wind trends do not masquerade as year-to-year variability.
  3. The detrended series is scaled to the fleet's actual operating wind speed and each year is run through the active power-curve pipeline, producing an annual energy series. The coefficient of variation of that series is the IAV used.

Computing in energy units directly avoids a known ambiguity in the literature: the widely-quoted UK figures (~6% onshore per Garrad Hassan, ~4–5.5% for UK waters per DNV's 2016 revision) are wind-speed standard deviations, and energy responds more than 1:1 to wind in the operating range. One UK study found a ~4% wind IAV corresponds to roughly a 7% capacity-factor IAV. When the fetch fails, those literature wind figures are applied as energy values directly (6% onshore / 4.5% offshore), clearly flagged as a fallback. This is a conservative-side simplification given the above.

Caveats: the reanalysis grid makes this a regional figure, which is appropriate because IAV is a synoptic-scale phenomenon (NAO-driven in northwest Europe) rather than a microscale one. It is not a substitute for an on-site long-term correlation (MCP) study.

11. Terrain model

A generalized wind climate (§5) describes flat ground. Hills, ridges and valleys are added back per turbine and per wind direction by a linear flow model run over an elevation grid around the site.

Elevation. The browser fetches AWS Terrain Tiles (the open Terrarium dataset first published by Mapzen) covering every turbine and any drawn boundary, with at least 4 km of margin all round, and resamples them onto a 512 × 512 grid in local metres. The spacing is the larger of 25 m and whatever the layout's extent needs, so a single turbine gets about 28 m and a layout 20 km across about 85 m. Tiles are cached in the browser, and moving turbines within the grid costs nothing. Sea and lakes below datum are treated as flat. Turbine elevations, slopes and RIX (§12) all come from the same grid. If the tiles cannot be reached, the backend's point elevation service and the old direction-blind 2H/L estimate are used instead, and the report says so.

Flow model. The model is a linearised solution of the Jackson and Hunt type, solved by Fourier transform over the grid in the manner of Mason and Sykes' three-dimensional extension. The best-fit plane is removed and the grid's outer 15% tapered, so the transform sees a continuous field. Each Fourier component of the terrain, with wavenumber k, then gives a fractional speed-up at height z above local ground of

ΔS(k) = h(k) · |k| · cos²α · exp(−|k|z) · G
G = [ln(L/z₀) / ln(max(z, l)/z₀)]²   below height L, otherwise 1

where α is the angle between the wavevector and the wind, L = 1/|k| is the component's length scale and l is its inner layer depth, from l·ln(l/z₀) = 2κ²L. The first part is the potential flow solution. G is the amplification near the surface, where the slower sheared flow responds more strongly to the same pressure field. Taking the outer reference speed at height L follows Jackson and Hunt's original analysis. Using the middle layer height instead (Hunt, Leibovich and Richards) read about a third low at Askervein, so it is not used.

Because cos²α expands into three fields that do not depend on direction, three inverse transforms give the speed-up for any wind direction. Each wind rose sector uses the average over its 30° width, and the result scales that sector's Weibull A for the turbine. z₀ is the roughness set in the Shearing tab. A value outside −50% to +100% is clipped, purely as a numerical guard.

Validation at Askervein. Askervein, a 116 m hill on South Uist, is the standard test case for this kind of model. With the tool's own terrain data and the 1983 measurements for winds from 210° at 10 m above ground (Taylor and Teunissen, as digitised for the Wind Energy Model Evaluation Protocol benchmark), the model follows the upwind slope and the summit ridge to within about 0.1 of speed-up (0.10 RMS over 30 masts on the live tool): 0.56 against 0.54 measured just below the summit, and 0.66 against 0.69 at the centre point. The hilltop itself reads 0.73 against 0.88, partly because the terrain data rounds off the narrow summit. Higher up the model meets the hilltop profile, at 0.45 against 0.44 at 34 m. On the lee side it fails as every linear model does: measured speeds drop by 35% to 65% where the flow separates, while the model predicts a small speed-up.

Limitations. Linear theory assumes gentle slopes, so speed-ups are overstated on steep ground and the lee side of hills is not represented. The response is the same for winds from opposite directions. There is no roughness change across the site, no forest canopy and no atmospheric stability, and outside a few countries the elevation data is a surface model, so trees count as terrain. This is a large step up from a point estimate, but it is not a substitute for a WAsP or CFD assessment in complex terrain.

12. Ruggedness (RIX) and map layers

Linear flow models lose accuracy where the ground is steep enough for the flow to separate. The standard measure of how much such ground surrounds a site is the ruggedness index RIX (Bowen and Mortensen): the share of 72 radial lines out to 3.5 km that is steeper than 30%. The tool computes it for each turbine from the terrain grid, sampling each line at the grid spacing.

A turbine with RIX above 5% shows a warning in its card, in the per-turbine results and in the report, and adds 3% to the resource uncertainty (§10). The 5% threshold is a judgement, not a standard. In WAsP practice the prediction error grows with the difference in RIX between the reference mast and the turbine, and a generalized climate has no reference mast, so the tool warns rather than applying a correction. RIX also depends on the resolution of the elevation data, and a 25 to 85 m grid reads somewhat higher than coarser maps.

Each turbine card also shows the slope at the turbine's base. Two optional map layers help with siting. Terrain slope shades ground at 10 to 20%, 20 to 30% and over 30%, with the last hatched. Hub height wind speed shows the site's GWA climate run through the flow model at the first turbine's hub height, with a readout under the cursor of the speed and its difference from flat ground. Open water is left blank on that layer, since the land roughness setting does not apply there.

13a. Power curve model

Every turbine uses the same generic curve shape, scaled by its rated power, rotor diameter and peak power coefficient Cp_max. From v15 the shape is a Cp-limited cube with a small fixed loss, rounded smoothly into rated power:

P(v) = Pr · x / (1 + xⁿ)^(1/n),   x = (½ρA·C·v³ − λ·Pr) / Pr

λ is a fixed drivetrain and electrical loss of 1.2% of rated power, and n = 4.1 sets how sharply the curve turns into rated. C is solved for each turbine so the finished curve peaks at exactly the Cp_max entered, which keeps the parameter meaning what its name says. Output is zero below cut-in and from cut-out. Nameplate rated wind speed is kept for reference but does not enter the curve.

Fit. λ and n were fitted to manufacturer power curves from the windpowerlib turbine library: sixteen Enercon and GE machines from 0.8 to 4.2 MW, with specific power from 220 to 600 W/m², each given its own peak Cp. At mean wind speeds of 5 to 10 m/s the fitted shape reproduces their annual energy to 1.3% RMS, and within about 4% in the worst case. An Adwen 5 MW curve, held out of the fit as a check, is matched about as well (2.2% RMS). Two library entries were left out: one whose curve implies a power coefficient above the Betz limit, which is a labelling error, and one exact duplicate.

The logistic curve used before v15 under-predicted the same set by about 9% on average, and by up to 37% for high specific power machines at low-wind sites, because it reached real output levels too late between 4 and 8 m/s. Earlier versions of this manual attributed the tool's high readings to the curve shape. The reference curves show the opposite, so that explanation is withdrawn. The new curve raises estimates by about 3% for a London Array type machine and about 7% for the 2.0 MW / 80 m class at an upland Scottish site.

Cp_max = 0.45 is applied to every library class. The reference curves peak between 0.45 and 0.52, so 0.45 sits at the conservative end. Cp_max is editable per turbine, and the turbine definition bench (§13) can stamp one specification onto every sited turbine at once.

The two fitted parameters contain information from the OpenEnergy Platform wind turbine library, distributed with windpowerlib, which is made available under the Open Database License (ODbL 1.0). The curves themselves are not distributed with WindyPins.

Cross-checks. Two operating UK farms serve as references, both run with real turbine positions from OpenStreetMap, the default Cp_max and the standard losses: London Array (175 × 3.6 MW / 120 m, offshore) and Braes of Doune (36 × 2.0 MW / 80 m, upland onshore). Under v14, London Array read two to four points above its published capacity factor and Braes of Doune nearly a quarter above its output before curtailment. Both were rerun under v15, with Braes of Doune at its believed 60 m hub height, and both still read high: London Array by a few points of capacity factor and Braes of Doune by around 30%. The power curve and terrain model each match their own references. At Braes of Doune the Global Wind Atlas's own map gives about 8.8 m/s at hub height, above the tool's 8.2 m/s, so the gap there traces to the wind climate itself rather than the terrain model. It is being investigated.

Comparisons of this kind need care over the basis. Published capacity factors are usually quoted after curtailment and against settled output, while this tool estimates energy at the turbine with only a small default curtailment allowance. Braes of Doune shows why that matters: public figures put its curtailment at roughly 15% of potential generation, so its headline capacity factor sits well below the quantity the tool estimates. These are sanity checks, not a calibration, and no parameter has been tuned to either site.

Custom turbines expose fully editable rated power, rotor diameter, cut-in/cut-out wind speeds, hub height, and Cp_max.

13b. Site area and grid fill

The site area tool draws one boundary polygon and fills it with a turbine grid.

Area. Enclosed area uses the spherical excess formula. Treating degrees of latitude and longitude as a uniform grid ignores the convergence of the meridians and overstates area by 1/cos(latitude), so about 55% at 50°N and a factor of two at 60°N. Once turbines sit inside the boundary the readout adds installed capacity and density in MW/km².

Grid construction. Spacing is given in rotor diameters and converted to metres using the selected turbine. The lattice uses the same axis convention as the spacing ellipses (§5): major spacing along the prevailing wind, minor spacing across it. With "align grid to prevailing wind" ticked the lattice rotates to the dominant sector of the wind rose, putting the rows across the wind. Unticked, the grid is north-up.

Alignment needs a wind rose, which needs a site centre. With no turbines placed, the automatic centre (§2b) sits on the boundary's area-weighted centroid, and drawing the boundary fetches its rose, so it is normally ready by the time the fill runs. A pinned centre is left where it is.

Fill modes. Regular is a rectangular lattice. Staggered shifts alternate rows by half the crosswind spacing, so each turbine sits between the two ahead of it instead of directly behind one. Hexagonal keeps that shift and closes the rows up until the diagonal neighbour sits at the along-wind design spacing, giving a row pitch of √(major² − minor²/4). Density rises while the closest pair stays as far apart as before: about 5% more turbines at the 5D × 3D default. Where major and minor spacing are equal the expression collapses to the familiar √3/2 hexagonal factor.

Filling and replacing. A fill replaces whatever is already inside the boundary, so re-filling at a new spacing never stacks two grids. Turbines outside are left alone by both fill and clear. Once placed they are ordinary sited turbines and can be edited, moved or deleted individually.

Two cases need a second click to confirm. The first fill against a boundary that already holds turbines reports how many it would remove, since that cannot be undone. Any fill above 50 turbines reports the count, because wake calculations get expensive quickly, with the square of the fleet size.

Terrain data. Filled turbines take their elevation and terrain speed-ups from the same grid as everything else (§11), so a large fill adds no network requests beyond any extra tiles. If the tiles are unreachable, the fallback point lookups are rate limited, and the fill reports how many turbines were left on flat ground.

Limitations. A fill covers the whole boundary, whereas a real layout uses only the buildable parts of a site once constraints, access and setbacks are applied. Filled densities therefore run higher than operating wind farms achieve, and the boundary is best read as a developable envelope. The grid takes no account of wake losses while placing, so it will not thin a downwind row or reshape the array. It uses one turbine type at a time and applies no exclusion zones, setbacks, slope or access constraints, though the slope layer (§12) shows where ground is steep. Boundaries crossing the antimeridian are not supported. Treat the output as a first draft to inspect and edit.

14. Reproducibility

Re-running an identical saved site gives identical results. Loading a site always builds a fresh terrain grid from its layout, so a save and reload against unchanged upstream data reproduces exactly. Within a session the grid is reused while the layout still fits inside it, and a grid rebuilt for a changed layout can move terrain speed-ups by a fraction of a percent.

Upstream providers occasionally update their datasets (GWA versions, terrain tiles, NASA POWER climatology), so results can shift slightly over months.

Validated against two operating UK farms (§13a). - Log-law shear extrapolation added alongside the power law (§3). - Direction-weighted Jensen wake using per-sector GWA Weibull data, with the upstream turbine's rotor diameter used in the deficit. - Weibull scale parameter derived with a proper gamma function (previously hard-coded for k=2 only). - Bulk elevation fetches queued (concurrency 3) after simultaneous bursts were found to be silently rejected by the free elevation service. - Terrain speed-up (Jackson & Hunt 2H/L, ±25% clamp) and slope-based terrain-complexity flag added (§11–12). - Deployment hardening: fixed backend URL, health-check/wake-up on load, longer fetch timeouts matched to free-tier cold starts.

v9 and earlier - Initial NASA POWER climatology pipeline, Weibull integration, omnidirectional Jensen wake, DNV/IEC-style multiplicative loss waterfall, P50–P99 exceedance, save/load site files, Netlify/Render deployment.

16. Roadmap

  • Next: roughness from land cover, sector by sector: an effective roughness for each sector from ESA WorldCover land cover upwind of each turbine, used to read the GWA climate per sector, so forest and coastline effects stop depending on one site-wide setting.
  • NEWA (New European Wind Atlas) as a candidate resource source, ~3 km mesoscale with hourly time series, potentially the terrain-aware hourly data neither GWA nor ERA5 provides alone. Gated behind a two-farm probe (NEWA 100 m means at London Array and Braes of Doune against GWA, ERA5 and the back-solved requirement), integrated only if it materially beats ERA5's −16% at the upland site.
  • Wake-added turbulence for deep-array recovery, the mechanism TurbOPark-class models add, aimed at the validated ~1–2 pp wake residual at London Array.
  • Exclusion zones within a site boundary (§13b), so a grid fill can respect setbacks, dwellings, steep ground and no-build areas rather than filling the whole envelope.
  • Manufacturer thrust curves in place of the analytic Ct(v) roll-off.
